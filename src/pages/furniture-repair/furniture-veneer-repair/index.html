What veneer is

Veneer is a thin sheet of wood glued over a solid base. It lets a cabinetmaker use beautiful figured wood across a whole dresser top without the top warping, and it has been used on fine furniture for centuries.

The weak point is the glue line. Most older veneered furniture was assembled with hide glue, which softens with heat and moisture. Over decades of humid East Texas summers and dry, air-conditioned winters, it lets go in patches: an edge lifts, a bubble rises in the middle of a top, or a corner snaps off and disappears.

The Conservation Center links lifted veneer, blisters and cracks to humidity swings that make old hide glue fail.

What you can safely do right now

  • Save every loose piece. Put chips and strips in an envelope taped inside a drawer. Original veneer matches better than any patch.
  • Do not pull or trim it. A lifted edge that is tugged or cut off turns a reglue into a patch.
  • Keep it from snagging. Lay a soft cloth or a sheet of paper over the area and avoid sliding things across it.
  • Avoid tape on the finish. Most tapes pull finish, and sometimes veneer, off when removed.
  • Keep it dry and away from heat. Move the piece off heating and cooling vents and out of direct sun.

This is shop guidance from our own work rather than a published standard, and it is the same thing we would tell a neighbor. The aim is simply to stop the damage spreading until the piece can be repaired.

The American Institute for Conservation recommends keeping furniture where relative humidity stays roughly between 40 and 60 percent and away from vents and heaters. That is the long-term fix that keeps new lifts from starting.

How a professional veneer repair is done

Regluing lifted veneer. The old glue under the loose area is cleaned out or softened so new glue can bond. At The Henry Ford, conservators traced 41 lifted areas on one chest to failed hide glue and laid fresh hot hide glue under them with a syringe and spatula.

Clamping evenly. Woodcraft advises clamping the area flat under a board, or caul, that covers the whole loose patch so it does not bubble, and leaving it clamped until dry, about a day for a blister.

Patching missing veneer. A new piece is cut from veneer matched for species, color and grain direction, shaped to the hole, glued in, leveled and colored to blend with the finish.

Finish touch-up. The repaired area is blended into the surrounding finish so it does not show.

When to bring it to a shop

Call a professional when: the piece is old, valuable or a family heirloom; the loose area is larger than your palm or in the middle of a top; pieces are missing; the veneer has bubbled in several places; or the surface also has water damage. Heat guns, irons and household glues used without experience can scorch veneer or leave glue that makes a proper repair harder.
