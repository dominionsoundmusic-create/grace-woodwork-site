Why tables wear out first

A table top is the one surface in the house that meets hot plates, cold glasses, elbows, homework and spilled tea every day. The finish on it wears faster than the finish on any other piece, and once it is worn through, water gets into the wood.

Table refinishing covers more than a new coat. The top is stripped and repaired, the base is checked and tightened, the leaves are matched to the rest of the table, and the new finish is chosen for a surface that gets wiped down after every meal.

We refinish dining tables, kitchen tables, drop-leaf and pedestal tables, coffee and end tables, and the occasional farm table that has spent years on a porch. If yours is a family piece with some age, read our antique furniture restoration page before you decide to strip it.

Common table problems and what fixes them

White or cloudy rings

Moisture trapped in the finish. Often treatable at the surface without a full refinish.

Black rings and stains

Water that reached the wood. Usually needs the finish off and the stain worked out of the wood.

Worn-through finish

Bare spots where hands and plates rest. A refinish puts protection back.

Cupped or split top

Uneven moisture or a top screwed down too tightly. Fixed by flattening or regluing and refastening so the wood can move.

Wobbly base

Loose joints at the aprons, legs or pedestal, taken apart and reglued.

Sticking leaves

Slides and pins cleaned, repaired or replaced so the table opens again.

Why tops crack, and how a refinished top avoids it

Solid wood swells and shrinks across its width as the humidity changes, and barely at all along its length. A top that is screwed tightly to its base cannot move, so in a dry season something gives: a glue line opens or the board splits.

Fine Woodworking describes several ways to fasten a top so it can move, such as wooden buttons in a slot or slotted screw holes. When we have a top off for refinishing, we check how it was attached and correct it if it was locked down.

Cupping, where a top bows up at the edges or the middle, often comes from one face taking on or losing moisture faster than the other, for example when the underside was never finished. A refinish that seals both faces evenly helps keep it flat.
