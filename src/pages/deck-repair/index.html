Why East Texas decks wear out

The Texas A&M Forest Service calls the Pineywoods the wettest part of the state, with most of the region getting 32 to 50 inches of rain a year. Add long hot summers and a deck lives a hard life: wet, then baked, then wet again.

Rot needs water. The USDA Wood Handbook says serious decay only happens when wood stays wetter than about 30 percent moisture, which is why it starts where water sits: board ends, the tops of posts, under planters and mats, and against the house. Termites look for the same damp wood; Texas A&M AgriLife notes that subterranean termites need contact with soil or another moisture source.

Most deck problems start small. A board softens at one end. A railing post loosens a little more each season. Catching them early keeps a repair to a repair.

Safe checks you can do yourself

The North American Deck and Railing Association (NADRA) publishes a simple checklist homeowners can follow. These checks only involve looking, pressing, pushing and probing with a screwdriver; leave anything that needs power tools or lumber to someone who repairs decks.

  • Probe for rot with a screwdriver in damp spots, board ends and around fasteners
  • Look at the ledger, the board where the deck meets the house, for rot, gaps or rust
  • Check the flashing over the ledger is in place and sending water away
  • Look for rusted or loose fasteners and connectors
  • Push on the railings; they should not move
  • Walk the stairs and look for sagging, loose treads or soft stringers

Stop and call if you find: a soft or rotten ledger, posts or joists; a deck that sways or sags; a railing that moves when you lean on it. These parts carry the load. Keep people off that area until it has been looked at.

NADRA itself says its checklist does not make a deck code compliant and recommends having a deck builder or home inspector evaluate it.

What we repair

  • Deck boards: rotten, split or cupped boards replaced, and loose ones refastened
  • Railings: loose posts resecured, broken balusters and top rails replaced
  • Stairs: worn treads replaced and loose steps tightened
  • Trim and skirting: fascia and lattice repaired or replaced
  • Finish: cleaning, then stain or sealer to protect the wood

When the problem is in the framing, the posts or footings, or the ledger connection to the house, that is structural work. We will tell you what we see, and where the job needs a structural assessment or a permit, we will say so before anything is quoted.

Replacement lumber outdoors is pressure-treated, and the American Wood Council advises treating every cut end and drilled hole, because the preservative does not reach the center of the board.
