Settled before the town existed

People were farming this ground from about 1848, mostly Southerners and largely Virginians, but no town formed until the railroad came through in the early 1870s. It was platted in 1873 where the line split, one branch to Tyler, the main line to Longview, and the post office opened as Zavala before the name changed to Troup in 1877.

By 1892 the town had six hundred people, eight general stores, four churches, a hotel, a cotton gin and gristmill. Families that arrived in that period frequently never left, and furniture that arrived with them frequently never left either. That is most of what comes to this shop from Troup.

Old pieces need the old methods

Nineteenth-century furniture was assembled with hide glue, cut joinery and hand-planed surfaces. Hide glue is reversible on purpose, a piece put together that way can be taken apart and repaired again in fifty years, which is exactly why those pieces are still here. Modern adhesives are stronger and permanent, which sounds better and is not, because the next repair becomes impossible.

On genuinely old work we keep to the old method, and the first question is always how little we can get away with doing.

What comes in

Loose and broken joints. Chairs that rock or have given way at the back. Drawers that bind, drop or sit crooked. Lifted and blistered veneer. Water rings, heat marks and sun-faded tops. Split boards and cracked legs. Missing moldings and turned parts cut to match rather than ordered. And pieces somebody already tried to fix once with the wrong glue, which is nothing to apologize for.

What not to strip

On something with real age the original surface is part of what it is worth, and taking it off cannot be undone. Cleaning, waxing and careful touching-in is frequently the better job and always the cheaper one. Where a piece looks valuable enough that it ought to be appraised before anything is done, we will say so rather than start work.
