Six historic districts means a lot of furniture worth keeping

Tyler has six National Register historic districts and two local overlay districts covering more than seventy properties. Charnwood was the city's first, known in the nineteenth century as South Tyler. The Brick Streets district runs back to the mid-1800s on the first paved streets in the city, full of Queen Anne, Colonial, Classical Revival and Craftsman houses. The Azalea district covers the first fifty-three years of the twentieth century, mostly Tudor, Colonial Revival and Spanish Revival, and was designated in June 2003.

Houses like that hold furniture that matches them. Pieces bought for the room a century ago, inherited with the house, or hunted down since to suit it. That is not furniture anybody replaces casually — which is why most of what comes to us from Tyler is a repair rather than a decision about whether to bother.

Victorian detail is where the skill goes

The Brick Streets houses are the ones that show it. Gingerbread work, fish-scale shingling, turned spindles, mansard detail — the same vocabulary appears on the furniture inside them. Repairing a broken turned leg or a run of missing spindles means cutting to match what is already there rather than fitting the nearest standard part, and a near-miss is obvious to anyone who lives with the piece.

The same applies to carved and applied detail. Where a section is gone entirely it has to be cut new; where it is loose it should be re-glued rather than replaced. The order of preference is always repair, then match, then remake — in that order, and we will tell you which one your piece needs.

What usually comes in

Loose and broken joints. Chairs that rock. Drawers that bind, drop or sit crooked. Lifted or blistered veneer. Water rings, heat marks and sun-faded tops — common in Tyler, where a lot of these houses have large south-facing windows and a piece has sat in the light for decades. Split boards, cracked legs, missing moldings. Full strip-and-refinish work when the finish genuinely is done.

Know what not to touch

On a genuinely old piece the original finish is part of what it is worth, and stripping it back cannot be undone. A clean, a wax and careful touching-in is often the better job and always the cheaper one. Where something looks valuable enough that it ought to be appraised before any work starts, we will say so rather than pick up a scraper.
