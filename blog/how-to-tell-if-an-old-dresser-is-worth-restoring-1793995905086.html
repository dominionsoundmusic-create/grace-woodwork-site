How to Tell If an Old Dresser Is Worth Restoring

A practical checklist for East Texas homeowners: what to inspect on an old dresser before you decide to restore it, repair it, or let it go.

Most people judge a dresser by its top. The top is the least useful thing to look at. Scratches, haze, and a worn finish are surface problems, and surface problems are the cheap part of any restoration. What matters is underneath and behind. Give yourself twenty minutes with a flashlight before you decide anything.

Pull a drawer and turn it over

Take one drawer all the way out and flip it. Look at the corners where the sides meet the front. Interlocking dovetails, whether cut by hand or by machine, tell you somebody built a real drawer box. Hand cut dovetails are uneven in size and spacing; evenly spaced machine cut ones show the piece is no older than about 1895, when dovetail cutting machinery came in. Either way, a dovetailed solid wood drawer can be knocked apart, cleaned, and reglued. Staples, hot melt glue, and a decorative front screwed onto a thin box are a different story.

While the drawer is out, look inside the case. Dust panels between drawers, drawer runners made of hardwood, and a back made of solid boards rather than thin hardboard all point to a piece built to be repaired. Also helpful: furniture built up through roughly the 1950s was usually assembled with animal hide glue, which softens with moisture and heat and is reversible. That means a woodworker can take a loose joint apart and reglue it properly instead of forcing more glue into a failing joint.

Figure out what it is actually made of

There is a big difference between solid wood, veneer over solid wood, and veneer over particleboard or fiberboard. The first two are almost always worth working on. Veneer laid with hide glue can be softened, re-glued, and pressed back down, and small missing patches can be filled in.

Particleboard and fiberboard are the ones to walk away from when they have gotten wet. Those panels are held together by bonds at countless small contact points, and moisture swells the material and ruptures those bonds. Thickness swelling can run past fifteen or twenty percent under bad moisture exposure, and it does not come back down. If the bottom edge of a side panel is puffed up and crumbly under your thumbnail, no amount of sanding fixes that.

Check the finish too. Put a drop of denatured alcohol somewhere hidden, like the inside of a leg. If the finish gets sticky and dissolves, it is shellac, which is common on older pieces and often can be amalgamated or recoated without full stripping. That alone can cut the labor on a project in half.

Watch for the real deal breakers

Wood constantly takes on and gives off moisture until it balances with the surrounding air. Furniture stock is typically dried to around seven to nine percent moisture content, and museums try to hold wooden objects between forty five and fifty five percent relative humidity. A dresser that spent fifteen summers in an unconditioned shed or barn outside Kilgore has swung far outside that range, over and over. That is where split tops, cupped drawer bottoms, and lifted veneer come from. Most of it is repairable, but it is work, and you should know it is there before you buy.

Two things are worse than movement. First is decay. A musty smell, black staining that goes into the wood rather than sitting on the finish, and wood that dents under thumb pressure all mean material has to be replaced, not cleaned. Second is powderpost beetles, which attack hardwoods because of the starch in the wood. Look for round exit holes about the size of a pinhead or pencil lead with fine powder, called frass, the color of fresh sawdust. Fresh powder means the infestation is likely active, and that piece should not sit next to other wood in your house until it is treated. Old holes with no powder are just history.

One more thing. Consumer uses of lead paint were banned in 1978, so anything painted before then should be treated as suspect. Do not dry sand it in the garage.

Doing the math

Write down what the piece actually needs: reglue the case, rebuild two drawers, replace a runner, patch veneer, strip and refinish. Then compare that against what a comparable new dresser costs. Solid wood, sound joinery, dry and bug free almost always comes out ahead, especially since a lot of older pieces in our area are built from local pine, which dents easily but repairs beautifully. Swollen particleboard, soft rotten rails, or active beetles usually do not pencil out unless the piece means something to your family, and that reason counts for plenty.
