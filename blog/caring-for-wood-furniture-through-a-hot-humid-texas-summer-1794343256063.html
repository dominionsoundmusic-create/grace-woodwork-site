Caring for Wood Furniture Through a Hot, Humid Texas Summer

Practical summer care for wood furniture in East Texas: humidity control, sun, sticking drawers, mold and beetle checks, and simple cleaning.

By the middle of July in East Texas, the air outside holds a remarkable amount of water, and every time a door opens, some of it comes inside. Wood notices. Wood is hygroscopic, which means a piece of furniture is always trading moisture with the air around it until it settles at what foresters call the equilibrium moisture content. When the air gets damper, the wood takes on moisture and swells. When the air dries out, it gives that moisture back and shrinks. The amount of movement tracks closely with the change in moisture content, and most of it happens across the grain rather than along the length of a board.

A common misunderstanding is that a good finish seals wood against all of this. It does not. Research from the USDA Forest Products Laboratory is clear that coatings slow the rate at which wood changes moisture content but never eliminate it, and that end grain absorbs moisture far faster than face grain. That is why the underside of a tabletop, the inside of a case and the raw bottoms of drawer sides matter just as much as the polished top you can see.

Steady air beats perfect air

Conservators who look after museum furniture generally aim for relative humidity in the 45 to 55 percent range, and they stress that rapid swings do more damage than a level that is a little high or a little low. You do not need museum conditions in a Kilgore living room, but the principle holds. A house that is cooled and dehumidified steadily all summer is kinder to furniture than one that is shut down to save money for two weeks and then chilled hard on the weekend.

A few things help. Buy an inexpensive hygrometer and set it where you keep your best pieces so you know what you are actually dealing with. If you leave town, set the thermostat up a few degrees rather than off. Building science guidance is to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, because once the humidity at a surface climbs toward 70 percent you are in mold territory. Pieces pushed flat against an exterior wall sit against the coolest surface in the room, which is where damp collects, so pull them out an inch or two and let air pass behind them. Uncooled garages, storage sheds and attics are the worst places in East Texas to park furniture you care about.

Sun and heat

Afternoon sun through a west window is harsh on a finish and on the wood underneath. It fades color unevenly, and conservation guidance also warns about direct sunlight simply for the heat it builds in a surface. Close blinds in the afternoon, or shift a piece out of the sun lane. Move lamps, runners and bowls around now and then so a tabletop does not tan in a pattern that only shows up when you finally move them. Sweating glasses and hot dishes are a summer problem too, so keep coasters and trivets handy.

Sticking drawers, loose joints, and uninvited guests

Drawers that glide in February can bind in August. That is swelling, and it usually reverses. Resist the urge to plane a drawer to fit on the most humid day of the year, because in January you will have a drawer that rattles. Wax on the runners is a better answer. On the other hand, joints that creak and chairs that rack are not seasonal, and summer swelling can hide a failing glue line until fall.

Give pieces a look underneath while you are at it. Fresh, flour-fine sawdust around pinholes about a sixteenth of an inch across points to lyctid powderpost beetles, which attack hardwood sapwood such as oak, ash, pecan and hickory, not the pine so much of our local framing and shop lumber comes from. Old holes with no fresh frass are history, not an active problem.

Simple cleaning and wax

Dust with a slightly damp soft cloth and dry it right away. Skip the heavy aerosol polishes and keep water off joints and veneer edges. A thin coat of quality paste wax once or twice a year on a clear-finished piece, buffed well, is the standard advice from museum care guides and it adds a little protection against handling and moisture.
