Refinishing vs. Restoring: What Each Does to an Antique's Value

How refinishing and restoring differ, what each does to an antique's value, and how East Texas heat and humidity factor into the decision.

People use "refinish" and "restore" like they mean the same thing. They don't, and the difference can be worth real money on an older piece.

Refinishing means taking the old surface off, stripping, sanding, and putting down a new coat. Restoring means repairing and stabilizing the piece while keeping as much of the original surface as you can: regluing joints, replacing a broken stretcher, touching in a worn spot, cleaning without scouring away the color. One resets the clock. The other keeps the clock running.

Why the old finish counts as part of the piece

The American Institute for Conservation no longer treats stripping and refinishing as standard practice on historic furniture. The reasoning is simple: the finish is original material, same as the legs and the drawer bottoms. It carries evidence of how the piece was made and how it was used, and once it's in the stripping tray it is gone for good. Conservators treat removing a finish as a last resort, after gentler options have failed.

That's not just a museum concern. The Canadian Conservation Institute makes the blunt point that roughly half the value of furniture, new or old, is in the finish. Patina, the layered color, the soft wear on arm rests and drawer pulls, the darker tone where a board was shaded by a molding, reads as age to anyone who knows furniture. Sand it off and you get a clean surface with no story, and buyers notice. A piece in honest, worn original finish usually brings more than the same piece stripped and sprayed.

So on anything that might have collector value, start with the assumption that you're restoring, not refinishing, and make refinishing prove its case.

When refinishing is the right call

Plenty of furniture has no originality left to protect. If a piece was already stripped in 1978 and shot with polyurethane, there's nothing historic about that coating. If the finish is flaking off in sheets, badly water-damaged, or the piece was painted over with wall paint, refinishing may be the only way to get back to something usable. The same goes for everyday family furniture you intend to use hard, a kitchen table that gets wiped down twice a day is not a museum object, and a durable new finish is a reasonable trade.

The middle ground gets overlooked. Furniture built before about 1920 usually carries a shellac finish, and shellac dissolves in denatured alcohol, which means a tired, crazed shellac surface can often be reamalgamated: the existing finish is softened with solvent and flowed back together rather than removed. Test in a hidden spot, like the underside of a rail. If the swab makes the finish tacky in a few seconds, you're likely looking at shellac and you have options short of stripping.

What East Texas does to old furniture

Wood takes on and gives up moisture until it balances with the air around it, and it moves as it does. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's guidance for interiors is roughly 30 to 50 percent relative humidity, which puts furniture wood near 6 to 8 percent moisture content. Our summers run well above that outdoors, and a house that swings from a humid, closed-up June to a dry, heated January works the joints every year.

Two consequences matter here. First, traditional hide glue is water-soluble and loses strength in sustained high humidity, which is why old joints give up in a climate like ours, that's a repair problem, not a finish problem, and stripping won't fix it. Second, high humidity and bare, unfinished wood invite powderpost beetles. Look for round exit holes, roughly 1/32 to 1/8 inch across, with fine flour-like frass sifting out. If you find fresh powder, isolate the piece, wrap it in plastic, and get advice before it spreads to anything else wooden.

Light matters too. Sunlight and UV craze and embrittle shellac and fade color unevenly, so keep antiques off the south-facing window wall. And skip the silicone-based spray polishes; the solvents and silicones in them can leave a milky bloom in an old finish and complicate any future repair. A soft cloth, occasional paste wax, coasters, and felt pads under glass tops will do more good than anything in an aerosol can.
