Wobbly Chairs: Common Causes and When a Repair Pays Off

How to find out why a wooden chair wobbles, which fixes make it worse, and when repairing a chair is worth the cost.

A chair that rocks under you is telling you something, and it pays to figure out what before you reach for the glue bottle. Most wobbles come from one of four places: loose joints, a leg that is slightly short or twisted, a cracked part, or hardware that has worked its way loose. Each one calls for a different fix, and a couple of them get worse if you guess wrong.

Find out what is actually moving

Set the chair on a hard, flat floor, not carpet. Press each corner of the seat in turn. If the chair tips back and forth on a diagonal but the frame itself feels solid, the problem is leg length or a frame that has racked out of square. If instead the whole chair shivers when you push on the back, the joints are loose. Narrow it down by holding one leg still and twisting the rung or rail next to it. Look at the seat corners for triangular glue blocks that have fallen off, and under the seat for metal braces with wandering screws.

Why wooden chair joints give up

Wood is always trading moisture with the air around it. Purdue Extension's work on shrinking and swelling notes that wood adsorbs moisture and swells when the air is humid, then shrinks when the air is dry, and that a change of about 6 percent in moisture content can change the size of a furniture part by roughly 2.5 percent. That sounds like nothing until you apply it to a tenon the size of your thumb. The same Purdue bulletin describes the exact failure most of us know: when a leg shrinks inside the pocket formed by two rails, the leg becomes loose and wobbly. Trouble also comes from parts that are mechanically restrained from moving. The National Park Service's guidance on wooden objects points out that when members are joined across the grain and held by glue, nails or screws, the stress can build until the wood splits.

East Texas gives wood plenty of practice at this. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory's Wood Handbook recommends matching wood to the moisture conditions it will actually live in, and indoor recommendations generally fall in the 30 to 50 percent relative humidity range. Our summers run well above that outdoors, and museum care guidance warns that levels consistently above about 70 percent can soften glues and encourage mold. A chair that spends July on a screened porch and January next to a heat vent is being worked hard.

One more cause worth ruling out. If you see small round holes in the wood, roughly 1/32 to 1/8 inch across, with fine flour-like powder sifting out, that points to powderpost beetles, which extension entomologists at Oklahoma State and the University of Maryland describe in those terms. A chair hollowed out from the inside is not a glue problem.

The quick fixes that make it worse

Driving a long screw through the outside of a leg into a loose rung feels decisive, but it splits turned parts, leaves a hole to patch, and does not stop the joint from working. Metal mending plates do the same thing on a bigger scale. Squirting glue into a closed joint rarely helps either, since the glue cannot reach the surfaces that matter. There is also a chemistry problem. Most chairs built before about 1950 were assembled with hot hide glue, which is reversible, and cured hide glue acts as a sealer that keeps new PVA glue from soaking into the wood fibers. The joint feels tight for a season, then lets go again. The real repair is to take the joint apart, clean the old glue off, restore the fit of the tenon in its mortise, glue it, and clamp it square.

When the repair is worth doing

A repair usually makes sense when the wood itself is sound, the chair is solid wood with real joinery, and all the parts are present. It makes even more sense when the chair belongs to a set, since matching one chair to five others is hard to do by shopping. Catching it early matters too, because a loose joint that keeps getting used wears itself oval, and then the fix involves building up or replacing the tenon instead of simply regluing it. Repair is a harder case when the chair is particleboard with cam fittings, when beetles or rot have taken the strength out of the wood, or when the frame is broken in several places at once. Even then, a piece with family history behind it can justify new parts cut to match the old.
