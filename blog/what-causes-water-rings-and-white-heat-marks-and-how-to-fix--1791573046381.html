What Causes Water Rings and White Heat Marks, and How to Fix Them

Why white rings and heat marks show up on wood tables, how to tell what you are dealing with, and the safest ways to fix them at home.

Somebody sets a sweating glass of tea on the dining table, and an hour later there is a pale ring staring back at you. Or a casserole goes down without a trivet and leaves a cloudy white patch. Both marks look alarming, and both are usually easier to deal with than people expect.

The mark is in the finish, not the wood

That milky white haze is almost never a stain. It is moisture trapped inside the clear coat of finish sitting on top of the wood. The moisture creates microscopic voids that scatter light instead of letting it pass through, so the finish loses its transparency and reads as white or gray. Finishers see the same effect as "blushing" when lacquer or shellac is sprayed on a humid day and turns milky. A white heat mark works the same way. Heat softens the finish film just enough for steam from a hot dish, or moisture already in the air, to get into it, and the haze stays behind as the surface cools.

This is good news, because white marks sit very near the surface of the film. Dark rings are a different animal. Brown, gray or black marks mean water went all the way through the finish and reacted with tannins in the wood itself. Those usually require stripping and bleaching, not a quick fix.

East Texas gives us plenty of chances to make white marks. Average relative humidity in our part of the state hovers around two thirds year round, so a cold glass sweats steadily from May through September. A lot of older pieces around here wear shellac or lacquer, which are more sensitive to moisture than modern polyurethane. And the pine furniture that is so common in this region is often finished thin, so water gets through quickly.

Figure out what you have before you touch it

Run a fingernail lightly across the mark and then across good finish beside it. If your nail glides over a smooth cloudy area, the damage is inside the film and you have options. If the nail catches on a ridge, a crater or a crack, the film is physically broken and heat or oil will not bring it back.

Next, find out what the finish is. Dab a little denatured alcohol on a hidden spot, like the underside of a leaf or the back edge of an apron. If it softens or gets sticky, it is shellac, common on pieces made before about 1920. If alcohol does nothing but lacquer thinner lifts it, think lacquer. If both shrug it off, you likely have a modern varnish or polyurethane. This matters because the right fix for one finish can ruin another.

Try the gentlest fix first

Start by doing nothing for a day. Fresh blush sometimes clears on its own as the moisture works back out.

If it is still there, try dry heat. A hair dryer on low, held several inches away and kept moving, is the safest tool in the house. You can also lay a folded cotton cloth over the mark and pass a warm, dry iron over it for a few seconds at a time. No steam, and check after every pass.

If heat does not finish the job, oil often will. Furniture polish, petroleum jelly or plain mayonnaise left on the spot overnight fills some of those tiny voids and restores part of the clarity. It rarely gets all of it, but it is harmless.

After that you are into abrading, which means rubbing the haze away with a mild abrasive such as toothpaste on a fingertip or 0000 steel wool lubricated with oil. This usually works, but it changes the sheen in that spot, so you may end up rubbing out the whole top to make it even again. Redissolving the finish, with alcohol on a pad for shellac or a commercial blush remover for lacquer, works fast in trained hands and goes wrong just as fast. Save that one for a piece you are willing to risk.

Two cautions. On thin oil or wax finishes, water raises the grain rather than clouding a film, and the fix is light sanding plus fresh finish. And skip the aerosol polishes with silicone. Silicone soaks into the wood and causes fish eye later, when someone tries to put a new finish on.

Keeping them from coming back

Coasters and trivets really are the whole answer. Add felt under lamps and planters, wipe spills right away rather than letting them sit, and keep a cloth handy in summer when glasses sweat.
