What's Inside Hardwood and Softwood, and Why It Matters

Hardwood and softwood describe tree biology, not toughness. Here is what that difference means for staining, dents, humidity and beetles at home.

The words hardwood and softwood sound like a grading system, as if one is tough and the other is flimsy. They are not. They are botanical labels. Virginia Cooperative Extension puts it plainly in its wood identification guide: the terms separate species taxonomically and have little to do with the actual hardness of the wood. Hardwoods are angiosperms, mostly broad leaved and deciduous. Softwoods are conifers, which in our part of the state means pine, mostly.

The numbers back that up. On the Janka scale, which measures the force needed to press a steel ball halfway into a board, eastern white pine comes in around 380 pounds force, while southern yellow pine such as longleaf runs near 870, close to the lower end of the oaks. White oak sits around 1,360 and hickory around 1,820. So a dense yellow pine board can outperform a soft hardwood, and a hardwood label on a price tag tells you less than you would think.

The difference is in the plumbing

The real distinction is cell structure. Hardwoods have vessel elements, which show up on a cut surface as pores. Softwoods have no vessels at all and move water through tracheids, which is why a planed pine surface looks closed and uniform under a lens while oak looks like a bundle of tiny straws.

That one fact explains a lot of what you see at home. Open pore hardwoods like oak, ash and pecan will never polish out glass smooth without a grain filler, because the pores stay as small valleys under the finish. It is also why stain pools in the grain lines of oak and gives it that striped look, and why dust and old polish collect there over the years.

Why pine blotches and oak usually does not

Pine grows in alternating bands of soft earlywood and dense latewood, and those bands drink stain at different rates. The result is the streaky, splotchy color most people have run into on a pine board. Finish manufacturers address this directly. Minwax says a pre stain wood conditioner is especially necessary on soft or porous woods such as pine, fir, alder, aspen, birch and maple, applied to clean bare wood sanded to 220 grit, to even out absorption of oil based stain. If the color already came out blotchy, their own guidance is to sand back to bare wood and start over with conditioner. Gel stains, which sit more on the surface, are another way around the problem.

Dents, humidity and what moves

A dent in a softwood tabletop is usually crushed fiber rather than missing wood, which is why felt pads under lamps and a cutting board under a knife do more good on pine than any finish will. Hard maple or oak will shrug off the same bump.

Both groups move with moisture, and that matters here. Air conditioning pulls a house dry while the outdoor air stays heavy, and furniture feels the swing. The Canadian Conservation Institute notes that film finishes, including natural resin finishes and paint, form a complete layer over the wood and are prone to cracking and loosening as the wood expands and contracts with changes in relative humidity. Their work also flags the weak spots: veneer over right angled joints, inlays and glued assemblies take the worst of repeated humidity cycles. Steadiness helps more than any target number, so keep good pieces away from supply vents, exterior doors and sun through a west window.

One pest that only eats hardwood

Lyctid powderpost beetles attack only large pore hardwoods, particularly oak, hickory, pecan and ash, and the females lay eggs in those open pores. They feed on starch in the sapwood, and they do not attack pine or spruce. Mississippi State Extension also points out that they cannot lay eggs in a finished wood surface, so the bare undersides, backs and drawer parts of hardwood furniture are where to look. Oklahoma State describes the signs: round exit holes about 1/32 to 1/16 inch across with flour or talc like frass falling out. If you find fresh powder, deal with it before the piece sits next to anything else wooden.
