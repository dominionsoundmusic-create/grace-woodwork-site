Refacing Kitchen Cabinets vs. Replacing: How to Decide

How to tell if your cabinet boxes are worth keeping, what refacing really involves, and when new cabinets are the smarter spend.

Most kitchen cabinet decisions get made by looking at the doors. That is backwards. The doors are the easy part to change. What decides whether refacing is money well spent is the condition of the boxes behind them, and in an East Texas kitchen those boxes have been living with summer humidity, dishwasher steam, and the occasional slow leak under the sink.

Refacing means the boxes stay put. The doors and drawer fronts come off and get replaced, the hardware is updated, and the parts of the box that still show, mainly the face frames and exposed end panels, get new veneer or a paint finish that matches the new fronts. Replacing means the boxes come out and new ones go in, which also opens the door to changing the layout. National cost guides put refacing an average kitchen somewhere in the range of five to thirteen thousand dollars, with full replacement typically running well above that. The price gap is real, but it only matters if the boxes deserve to stay.

Check the boxes before you fall in love with a door style

Open the sink base and look at the bottom panel and the back corners. Then check the cabinet next to the dishwasher and the toe kicks. If you see swelling, soft spots, or a surface that looks puffed up along the edges, you are almost certainly looking at particleboard that got wet. That matters because when composite panels absorb free water, the thickness swell is largely irreversible. The panel does not shrink back when it dries. Sanding and painting over a swollen panel hides it for a season and no longer.

Next, push sideways on a face frame and watch the box. If the cabinet racks or has pulled away from the wall or the neighbor cabinet, the problem is the carcass or the fasteners, not the door. Then look at the hinge screws. Particleboard holds screws considerably worse than solid wood of similar density, which is why hinge holes in older budget boxes strip out and keep stripping out. One or two can be fixed. A kitchen full of them is telling you something.

Finally, pull a drawer all the way out and look at how it is built. Drawer boxes can be rebuilt independently, so a worn drawer is not by itself a reason to replace a kitchen.

When refacing makes sense, and when it does not

Reface when the boxes are plywood or sound particleboard, they are square and solidly attached, and you like where everything sits. You are buying a new look, new hardware, and often better doors than the originals, without tearing out the kitchen.

Replace when you want the layout changed, when water has already swollen the sink base or dishwasher run, when boxes are racked or sagging, or when you want full access drawers in base cabinets that were never built for them. Also replace if the interiors are failing. Refacing does nothing for a shelf that bows under a stack of plates.

There is a middle path people forget. You can keep good boxes, replace only the doors and drawer fronts, rebuild or retrofit drawers, repair the one or two water damaged panels, and refinish the face frames. That is a sensible route when most of the kitchen is fine and one corner is not.

Two details that decide how long a reface lasts

Adhesion is the first. Veneer applied over face frames with ordinary contact cement can bubble or release over time, especially under heat and when a solvent finish goes over the top. Ask what adhesive and what veneer thickness a contractor uses, particularly for the end panel beside a range or the frame next to a dishwasher. Paper backed veneer stuck down casually in a hot, humid kitchen is a callback waiting to happen.

Prep is the second. Kitchen cabinets carry a film of cooking grease that no primer will stick to. Degrease first with a TSP solution or a similar cleaner, rinse, let it dry completely, then scuff sand and prime. Grease also clogs sandpaper, so cleaning has to come before sanding, not after.

Afterward, keep the kitchen in the range wood likes. Indoor wood settles around six to eight percent moisture content, and holding indoor relative humidity roughly between thirty and fifty percent keeps that steady. Run the range hood and the bath fans, crack the dishwasher only after the drying cycle has cooled, and fix a drip under the sink the week you notice it.
